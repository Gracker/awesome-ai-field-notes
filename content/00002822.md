## English Summary

Architect Labs introduced Frostbite, a general-purpose CPU with a new instruction set built on interaction nets, discovered, designed, and verified end-to-end by its AI systems. Programs are graphs rewritten through local port rewrites; agent swarms iterated compiler, ISA, architecture, microarchitecture, RTL, verification, and GDS with a formally backed ASL contract as the single source of truth, Lean contracts (31 blocks), and an Arcmodel simulation 660x faster than RTL. Across 25 workloads — 15 held out until the final evaluation — Frostbite reaches a geometric mean of 4.59x the TCPU's performance-per-watt (6.02x on search-set, 3.83x on held-out). Programs are written in Vine (Rust-like syntax) and compiled to the Ivy IR.

Abstract In our previous post, Redwood , we showed that given an architecture, our AI systems could implement, verify, and deploy an inference accelerator in record time. However, similar to other scientific domains, we believe that AI systems, given the correct freedom and guardrails, can co-design software and hardware together, making optimizations across the stack and opening a design space so large it was previously inaccessible. We set out to answer the question: “Could our AI systems & tools lead to a novel hardware discovery that radically outperforms human-made designs?” Today, we're introducing Frostbite, a general-purpose CPU with a new instruction set built from the ground up. Frostbite exploits the properties of interaction nets, namely that every step is local, every dependency is explicit, and every value has exactly one owner, to reach a step-function gain in CPU performance that has long stagnated. On a set of 25 initially chosen workloads, including 15 held out until the final evaluation, we achieved a 4.59x performance-per-watt multiple when compared to a modern, silicon-proven superscalar (dual-issue, in-order) CPU core. Our AI systems autonomously iterated over the compiler, ISA, architecture, microarchitecture, RTL, verification, and final GDS. When we took a closer look at the discovered architectural primitives, we were surprised to find novel hardware implementations and runtime features that would have otherwise been difficult to architect. In spite of stagnation in CPU core improvement, we were able to take a workload concept all the way to a final GDS, and prove a step-function increase in perf/W and perf/area. We believe that as AI systems become more capable, every class of chip we know today, from datacenter-grade CPUs, to AI accelerators, to networking switches and NICs, will go through similar fundamental architectural shifts. Introduction to interaction nets Mainstream programming languages, when compiled for general-purpose CPUs, hide the dependencies between the parts of a program. Any piece of code may read or write any variable through a shared, mutable state, and so modern CPU hardware assumes the worst case and rediscovers, at runtime, which instructions actually depend on each other. Moreover, single-core CPU performance has largely stalled since the early 2000s, pushing people towards large multicore CPUs such as Intel Xeon, AMD Zen, and NVIDIA Vera. This forces CPUs to maintain the illusion that every core sees the same memory, and keeping that illusion consistent and performant requires deep cache hierarchies and coherence protocols that cost area and power, and scale poorly. Interaction nets 1 take a different approach. A program is a graph of nodes, and computation happens by rewriting pairs of nodes that are connected through their ports. Every rewrite only touches a small, local part of the graph. Because each node has exactly one principal port, no two of these active pairs ever overlap, so they can be rewritten in any order, or all at once, and the result is always the same. Interaction nets are also linear: every value is used exactly once, so there is no shared mutable state for the hardware to guard. Languages such as Vine 2 compile ordinary-looking programs directly to interaction nets. For an excellent introduction to interaction nets and why they suit hardware, we highly recommend Tendrils' “On Interaction Nets.” 3 Fig. 1. Five queens, two ways. On the left, a C++ program walks the search tree one step at a time. On the right, the same search as an interaction net explores many branches at once: 3,183 interactions with a critical path of only 262 steps. Fig. 1 shows the difference on a small search problem, placing five queens on a five-by-five board. The sequential program visits one candidate at a time. The interaction net exposes all of its independent work: on average, about twelve interactions are ready to run at every step. Nothing about the program was rewritten to get this parallelism; the dependencies were simply never thrown away. In theory, this should have two large advantages. The first is multithreading for free: rather than varying levels of coarse and fine-grained parallelism, parallelism comes straight from the program. No threads, locks, or race conditions, and none of the out-of-order machinery a conventional core uses to dig it back out. The second is that, since every value has one owner and every step is local, data can live in small local memories next to where it is used. That removes much of the area and power a conventional chip spends on large caching subsystems, and much of the system-level complexity of keeping those caches coherent.
