Addy Osmani is a big fan of agentic workflows - and warns that nothing in the day-to-day forces critical thinking anymore. He describes a spectrum from cognitive offloading through deferral (the tool's output becomes your new input, no longer a hypothesis) to cognitive surrender (Steven Shaw and Gideon Nave's term: the AI's output quietly becomes your own). ## 原文


## 原文摘录（X Article, 2026-11-03）

In the past, most of our cognitive and aesthetic processing happened in the space between question and answer. That space is now measured in seconds. The more you use agentic workflows, the more difficult it becomes (though not in the way I think people expected): as the models improve, the percentage of obvious errors is lower. Many (most?) outputs will be well-written and mostly correct, but they won't demand a second look.

Even if you are running many agents at a time, you still only have one pair of eyes on the outputs.

At the top: cognitive offloading, the act of delegating tasks to the AI, but maintaining ownership of the result. In the middle: deferral, where the output of the tool becomes your new input; the result has ceased to be a hypothesis. At the bottom: cognitive surrender - the AI's output quietly becomes your own, you feel no need to check it.

Did I formulate a hypothesis before asking? Before I run an agent on any non-trivial task, I write down, even just in my head, what I think the answer might be. What would have to be true for this to be wrong? Then go find out. A model's self-assessment of confidence is much less valuable than an actual test you can perform, so give it one.

Would I bet on this in front of the person who has to maintain it? This applies to code and to anything else that you're delivering. I believe that this is the bar for answerability.
