## Source Highlights

- Late last month, more than 300 representatives from major media outlets were on Capitol Hill to advocate for the Stealth Bot Prohibition Act. Newsrooms are being hit by a surge of automated traffic, mostly from AI developers, who extract content and then potentially deprive site owners of referral traffic and ad revenue — a real problem deserving a real solution.
- But the bill doesn't directly target the harms caused by abusive crawling. Instead, it would force anyone accessing a website automatically to identify themselves and their purposes for collecting public information.
- Cutting-edge investigative journalism relies on automated data collection: a Pulitzer-winning 2022 Wall Street Journal investigation analyzed over 31,000 financial disclosures from 12,000 senior government employees, relying on web scraping.
- The authors argue that bills purporting to target stealth crawling would hamper legitimate public-interest activities including preservation, journalism, research, and security — and that breaking the open web won't stop the bad bots.

## English Summary

Tech Policy Press argument on the Stealth Bot Prohibition Act: more than 300 media representatives lobbied on Capitol Hill, responding to a real surge of AI-developer crawlers that extract content and starve sites of referral traffic. But the bill targets identification rather than the actual harms of abusive crawling, and threatens the automated data collection that investigative journalism, preservation, and security research rely on — exemplified by the Pulitzer-winning WSJ analysis of 31,000 financial disclosures from 12,000 officials, which depended on scraping. The piece argues aggressive large-scale scraping is a real problem deserving real solutions, but blanket mandatory identification of every automated access would hamper legitimate public-interest work more than it stops the bad bots it names.
