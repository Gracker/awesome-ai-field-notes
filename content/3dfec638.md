# I told you so ([permalink](https://pluralistic.net/2026/11/13/outlookpocalypse/#bright-line-test))

Here's the one weird trick that made it possible for monopolies to conquer the world: the antitrust bar changed the rules so that it was impossible to prove whether a company had formed an illegal monopoly, and then these rules meant that monopolies were almost never challenged, and what challenges did arise were usually unsuccessful, and even the successful challenges resulted in meaningless "penalties" that left monopolists unscathed.

Every policy has three important dimensions: substance, scope and administrability:

-   "Substance" is what the policy actually says. For example, the basic substance of copyright law is, "You can't display, transmit or reproduce a copyrighted work without permission."

-   "Scope" is when the policy gets applied. When copyright law was formulated, the key activities it regulated all required a bunch of expensive industrial equipment. Every book implied a printing press, every record implied a record-pressing plant, every film implied a film duplication lab. That meant that, by default, pretty much the only people who had to care about what copyright law said were industrial actors – firms that might reasonably be expected to also have access to a lawyer. That matters a lot to administrability.

-   "Administrability" is what it takes to actually _administer_ the policy. Can you detect potential violations, and when you do, how hard is it to figure out whether the rule was actually broken? Again, in the pre-digital era, it was pretty easy to spot copyright violations: pirate books, records and movies are tangible, physical objects, and distributing them requires advertisement, delivery and payment.

After detecting an infringement, copyright enforcers must resolve a further question: whether a display, transmission or copy falls under "fair use." Fair use is a "fact intensive" rule, one that requires a lot of evidence and argument to adjudicate. It's almost impossible to categorically rule in or rule out a specific use as "fair":

[https://pluralistic.net/2024/06/27/nuke-first/#ask-questions-never](https://pluralistic.net/2024/06/27/nuke-first/#ask-questions-never)

Despite the complexity and expense of determining fair use, pre-digital copyright was still relatively administrable, because fair use questions themselves were relatively rare. Most fair uses (for example, photocopying part of a book for classroom use) were below the threshold for detection by rightsholders, so these questions almost never found their way into court. The fair use questions that did get to court were big, splashy affairs, like whether it's okay to rewrite a famous pro-slavery novel from the enslaved characters' perspective (it is!):

[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The\_Wind\_Done\_Gone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wind_Done_Gone)

There are a lot of legal questions that require nuance, fact-finding and argumentation to resolve, and that's not necessarily a problem. It's fine to apply these complicated, lengthy processes to things that only happen every once in a while. Take probate: most of us will die one or fewer times, so it's not the worst thing in the world that figuring out what happens to our stuff when that happens can involve some legal complexity.

But as media went digital, copyright's scope expanded dramatically. "Making or handling copies of copyrighted work" stopped being an industrial activity that implied a complex business and access to legal expertise. The only thing a digital computer _does_ is make and handle copies, so every time any ordinary person clicks their mouse, dozens of potentially thorny copyright questions arise. What's more, those questions involve the public internet, which raises the number of potential infringements that a rightsholder can detect (and potentially seek legal redress for) from dozens to millions.

Nearly all of these potential infringements fall under fair use, but fair use is a fact-intensive doctrine suited to resolving occasional disputes between large firms. Fair use is monumentally unsuited to resolving questions that arise hundreds of times every day for nearly every person alive, including small children and other people who almost certainly can't get legal advice:

[https://locusmag.com/feature/cory-doctorow-a-new-deal-for-copyright/](https://locusmag.com/feature/cory-doctorow-a-new-deal-for-copyright/)

This is how copyright broke: new technology (digital computers) radically expanded its scope, conscripting everyone into using copyright's framework for everyday activities that had nothing to do with the media industry's supply chain. That framework was developed for industrial actors with access to lawyers, and its most important aspect (fair use) is a fact-intensive doctrine that takes serious work to resolve, but now had to be navigated many times every day by every person.
