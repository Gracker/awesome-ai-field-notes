> **Editor’s Note:** The following situation is extremely fast-moving and it’s likely that more information gets reported both after I finish writing it and after it goes to press. As a result, the story may change in unexpected ways.

I’m not gonna lie — it’s been one of the most enjoyable weeks of writing in my entire life. [As I discussed in this week’s free newsletter](https://www.wheresyoured.at/jensens-bizarre-adventure/), Jensen Huang's tryst with an alleged spy running a front for the Chinese to buy GPUs is just the tip of the iceberg of an NVIDIA-backed smuggling ring, one that may, [per Culper Research](https://culperresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Culper_NVDA_5-13-2026.pdf?ref=wheresyoured.at), account for more than 20% (over $43 billion) of NVIDIA’s Fiscal Year 2026 revenue.

[And now Jensen Huang is no longer CEO of NVIDIA](https://www.nytimes.com/live/2026/11/12/business/nvidia-jensen-huang-resigns?eafs_enabled=false&ref=wheresyoured.at), replaced by CFO Colette Kress.

I also cannot express how many notes, connections, and sidequests got left on the cutting room floor as I wrote this week’s free piece piece, which may seem surprising given that it was over _7800 god damn words._ Megaspeed — and its connected entities — represents a sprawling, multinational smuggling operation that goes far beyond NVIDIA, Jensen, Alice, or even China. And, more fundamentally, it’s just _weird_.

Up until as recently as _last week_, talking about NVIDIA’s illicit shipments of GPUs would have seen you branded a conspiracy theorist or a “doomer,” mostly because the allegations are so severe, but also because NVIDIA’s bull run was so impressive and rapid, the idea that any meaningful part of it was the result of anything underhand simply couldn’t register with the most dented cheerleaders for the AI bubble.

And yet, none of this should have been a surprise. Culper’s research — which I referenced heavily in the free piece — was published in May 2026, and it formed part of the basis for its short position. And it needs to be stressed how significant this is, and how much peril Culper would find itself in if it turned out to be wrong — and, therefore, how confident it was in its findings.

If Culper was wrong, it didn’t open itself up to potential civil action (US defamation law sets a higher bar than in other countries, like the UK or Australia, but it still exists), but also, if you’re wrong when making a short, the losses are _theoretically infinite_.

The Wall Street Journal’s piece ([that ran on Wednesday](https://www.wsj.com/business/nvidia-short-seller-culper-jensen-huang-0e0cec38?mod=hp_lead_pos9&ref=wheresyoured.at)) fails to square the circle of how the largest media outlets in the world ignored the story entirely, yet the most damning part is that _even in accepting that Culper might be onto something, the Journal fails to mention that 20% of NVIDIA’s Fiscal Year 2026 revenue might have come from China, nor does it make any attempts to discuss the contents of the report._

In short, it treats Culper’s research — and the Jensen Huang infidelity — as a sort-of turbocharged tabloid scuttlebut, rather than looking at the bigger picture, which is that the (intermittently) biggest company in the world had lied about how and where it makes its money, and whether a significant chunk of its revenues came from illicit sources.

That’s because (like so much in the AI bubble) once you actually consider the contents — the scale of the smuggling, the massive amounts of “sanctioned” silicon that still makes it to China, and the amount of American counterparties (like Super Micro) involved — it becomes clear that _NVIDIA has repeatedly and unashamedly profited from breaking the law._

Nobody wants to talk about the elephant in the room — that NVIDIA is ultimately a dodgy company that misleads its customers, investors and the general public, and I believe that the business and tech journalism ecosystem has helped it varnish its reputation again and again.

People are so quick to forget that [NVIDIA was fined by the SEC _less than four years ago_](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2022-79?ref=wheresyoured.at) for misleading investors in 2017 (Fiscal Year 2018, beginning in February 2017), when it claimed that its surge of revenue growth came from gamers buying graphics cards rather than _cryptocurrency mining operators._

Per the SEC:

> In two of its Forms 10-Q for its fiscal year 2018, NVIDIA reported material growth in revenue within its gaming business. NVIDIA had information, however, that this increase in gaming sales was driven in significant part by cryptomining. Despite this, NVIDIA did not disclose in its Forms 10-Q, as it was required to do, these significant earnings and cash flow fluctuations related to a volatile business for investors to ascertain the likelihood that past performance was indicative of future performance.

And just like with the AI bubble, some members of the media fell over themselves to sell the narrative to investors, [unrepentantly repeating Jensen’s fictitious narrative about gaming driving its GPU sales](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/nvidia-corp-reports-546-percent-rise-in-quarterly-profit-841527?ref=wheresyoured.at), with [GamesBeat printing one of the most damning quotes in history](https://gamesbeat.com/nvidia-ceo-cryptocurrency-ai-and-the-leisure-economy/?ref=wheresyoured.at):

> GamesBeat: Did you say a hallelujah for cryptocurrency?
> Jen-Hsun Huang: No? Cryptocurrency is around. But it represented only a couple hundred million dollars, maybe $150 million or so. There’s still crypto mining to go to, currency mining. It comes and goes. It’ll come again. Somebody will come up with new hardware. Digital currency is not going away. It’s here to stay.
> GamesBeat: So it’s helpful, but it’s not a gold rush that Nvidia is selling the picks and shovels for?
> Huang: No, not a gold rush per se. We’re not opposed to it. But our core business is elsewhere.

It turned out this was a bag of bollocks.

This isn’t the only time the SEC has slapped NVIDIA around.
