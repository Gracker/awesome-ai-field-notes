# Landmark German Ruling: Google's AI Overviews Are Google's Own Words and Google Is Liable for False Answers

- **ID**: 6222b54e
- **来源**: the-decoder.com (Matthias Bastian)

- **抓取时间**: 2026-07-01

## English

### Key Points

- A German regional court has ruled that Google is directly liable for false claims in its AI-generated search overviews.
- In this case, Google's AI had wrongly linked two publishers to scams and shady business practices.
- The court treated the AI overviews as Google's own content and rejected Google's argument that users were responsible for fact-checking the results themselves.

### Google's statement (added June 11, 2026)

"We invest deeply in the quality of AI Overviews to ensure that the overwhelming majority of responses provide accurate information, and they are designed to reflect the information that exists on the web. We're carefully reviewing this decision, which is not yet final." Google argues that AI overviews can occasionally miss context or misinterpret web content, just like traditional search results. But that's exactly where the Munich ruling disagrees.

### Original article (June 9, 2026)

The Regional Court of Munich hit Google with a temporary injunction barring the company from spreading false claims about two Munich-based publishers through its AI-generated search overviews (case no. 26 O 869/26). The court classified Google as a direct infringer because the "AI overview" is its own content, not just a list of search results.

Google's AI overviews had falsely tied two publishing companies to scams, subscription traps, and shady business practices for certain search queries. According to the court, the AI mixed up information about other, genuinely sketchy companies with the plaintiffs and drew connections that didn't appear in any of the linked sources. The publishers sent Google a cease-and-desist letter, but Google didn't respond appropriately.

### AI overviews aren't search results

Google's AI overviews work nothing like traditional search results, the court argues. The AI rewrites and judges results "in its own words and according to its own structure." In the case at hand, for example, it opened with confident claims like "Yes, [company] is known for dubious business practices," then built its own structure with a summary, red flags for the alleged scam, and tips for users.

The court also found that the AI overview made claims "that are not even made in the search results." None of the linked sources drew any connection between the plaintiffs and the shady companies the AI mentioned. The court called these "the defendant's own statements." Google built the AI, Google offered it to users, so Google owns what it produces, "because it alone has influence over the AI's offering and the algorithms with which the AI operates."

### Search engine liability rules don't apply to AI "search"

The court also examined existing rulings from Germany's Federal Court of Justice (BGH), which gave traditional search engines and autocomplete limited liability. The BGH had argued that search engine operators were only liable as indirect infringers because they merely made third-party content findable. A proactive duty to check results would threaten how search engines work.

The Munich court found that this reasoning doesn't apply to AI overviews. A regular search engine just points to outside websites. But AI overviews generate "independent, new, and substantive statements" by evaluating and combining content from various third-party sites. And only Google can check those statements, "at least by comparing the underlying third-party websites with its own statements based on them."

The court also noted that the AI overview is "by no means absolutely necessary" for using the internet. Traditional search results already help users sort through information; the AI overview is just an extra feature.

### Google's "users can check for themselves" defense falls flat

At the hearing, Google argued that users could check the linked sources themselves to verify whether the AI summary was correct. Users generally knew "that information generated with AI should not be blindly trusted." That's a remarkable statement given the scale at which Google serves AI overviews. It's also not entirely true, since the connection between sources and generated content isn't always there.

The court rejected this. The possibility of disproving a statement through further research doesn't "regularly exempt from liability for this statement." The AI overview was "understandable on its own" and contained "a self-contained statement with independently understandable content and no reference to other possible interpretations or even unreliable content." Studies show that users almost never click on sources in AI overviews, which supports the court's reasoning.

The court drew a parallel to press law, where publishers are liable for teasers that are understandable on their own, even if readers never read the full article. Google's own argument would also "significantly diminish" the benefit of the feature, the court noted, if the overview were "generally recognized as unreliable."

The court also pointed to a protection gap: if Google were only liable for obvious violations, victims would have no real legal recourse when the AI makes false claims. The third parties whose websites served as sources hadn't even made the statements in question. So victims couldn't sue the sources, and under existing rules they couldn't effectively sue Google either. As a result, Google couldn't invoke host provider protections under the Digital Services Act or fall back on the standard notice-and-take-down process for search engines.

### AI-generated opinions get less free speech protection

As if the rest wasn't bad enough for Google, the court also went after free speech protection for AI-generated content. An AI's opinion is "not the expression of an acquired conviction of the persons expressing it, but the result of an algorithm." Offering AI-powered research is "above all an expression of Google's business activities" and "at most a secondary expression of an interest in being able to freely express one's opinion and beliefs." When weighing the plaintiffs' privacy rights against Google's interests, Google had to take a back seat, especially since the challenged statements were based on untrue facts.

### Google picks up 80 percent of the legal tab

The court ruled in favor of the plaintiffs on most counts. It banned claims about scams, connections to dubious companies, subscription traps, phone calls that never happened, and lack of availability. Only two minor requests got denied. The risk of repeated violations remained, even though the specific texts were no longer being displayed. Google covers 80 percent of the legal costs; the plaintiffs pay 10 percent each. The ruling may also have international reach.

### Even a 91 percent accuracy rate means millions of wrong answers

The Munich ruling goes far beyond this one case. An analysis by AI startup Oumi for the New York Times found that Google's AI Overviews with the current Gemini 3 model answered correctly 91 percent of the time. That's solid enough for everyday use by most people. But at Google's scale, it still means millions of wrong answers every hour. If enough of that wrong content defames companies or individuals, it could become a serious legal problem not just for Google but for other providers of similar services like ChatGPT, Claude, or Perplexity.

The Oumi analysis also found that 56 percent of the correct Gemini 3 answers couldn't be backed up by the sources Google linked. The AI is giving answers whose origins users can't trace. The Munich court tackled exactly this problem: the AI makes its own claims that don't appear in any linked source, and the operator has to answer for them. Whether this reasoning holds up on appeal remains to be seen. But if it gains traction internationally, the fallout could hit not just Google but every AI provider whose systems paraphrase content from the web.

## 中文

### 要点

- 德国一家地区法院裁定，Google 须为其 AI 生成的搜索概览（AI Overviews）中的虚假陈述承担直接责任。
- 本案中 Google 的 AI 错误地将两家出版商与诈骗、可疑商业行为挂钩。
- 法院把 AI 概览视为 Google 自己的内容，驳回了"用户应当自行核实"的辩护。

### Google 声明（2026 年 6 月 11 日新增）

Google 发言人表示："我们对 AI Overviews 的质量投入巨大，以确保绝大多数回答提供准确信息，且 AI 概览的设计初衷是反映网络上已有的信息。我们正在仔细研究这一尚未生效的判决。"Google 同时称 AI 概览偶尔会像传统搜索结果一样漏掉上下文或误读网页内容——而这正是慕尼黑法院不同意的部分。

### 原文（2026 年 6 月 9 日）

慕尼黑地区法院对 Google 发出临时禁令，禁止其通过 AI 生成的搜索概览继续散布针对两家慕尼黑出版商的虚假陈述（案号 26 O 869/26）。法院将 Google 认定为直接侵权人，因为"AI 概览"是 Google 自己的内容，而不仅仅是一份搜索结果清单。

Google 的 AI 概览在特定搜索查询中，把两家出版公司错误地与诈骗、订阅陷阱、不良商业实践挂钩。法院认为，AI 把其他确实可疑的公司的信息与原告混在一起，并建立了原始链接来源中根本不存在的关联。出版商曾向 Google 发送停止侵害函，但 Google 未作出恰当回应。

### AI 概览不是搜索结果

法院指出，AI 概览的运作方式与传统搜索结果完全不同。AI"以自己的措辞和结构"重新组织和判断结果。以本案为例，AI 概览以"是的，[某公司]以可疑商业行为闻名"这种武断的口吻开头，然后自行构建"摘要—红旗—用户建议"的篇章结构。

法院还发现，AI 概览中的陈述"在搜索结果中根本没有出现过"。所有被引用的来源都没有把原告与 AI 所提到的可疑公司联系起来。法院将这些陈述称为"被告自己的言论"。Google 训练了这个 AI，由 Google 提供给用户，所以 Google 须为其产物负责——"因为只有 Google 能影响 AI 的供给及其运行所用的算法。"

### 搜索引擎的责任豁免不适用于 AI "搜索"

法院还审视了德国联邦最高法院（BGH）此前对传统搜索引擎和自动补全给出的有限责任规则。BGH 当时认为，搜索引擎运营商只能作为间接侵权人承担责任，因为他们只是让第三方内容"可被找到"；课以主动审查义务会威胁搜索引擎本身的运作。

慕尼黑法院认为该推理不适用于 AI 概览。普通搜索引擎只是指向外部网站，但 AI 概览通过评估和组合多个第三方网站的内容，生成"独立、新颖且具有实质意义的陈述"。而且只有 Google 能核查这些陈述——"至少可以将底层第三方网站与基于它们生成的陈述进行比对。"

法院还指出，AI 概览"对于使用互联网绝不是不可或缺的"。传统搜索结果已经足以帮助用户筛选信息，AI 概览只是一个附加功能。

### Google"让用户自行核实"的辩护被驳回

庭审中 Google 主张，用户本可以自行查阅链接来源以核实 AI 摘要是否正确；用户通常也知道"不应盲目信任 AI 生成的内容"。考虑到 Google 推送 AI 概览的规模，这一说法相当引人注目。而且它并不完全成立，因为来源与生成内容之间的对应关系并不总是存在。

法院驳回了这一论点。能够通过进一步研究反驳某陈述，并不"通常地豁免就该陈述的责任"。AI 概览"本身即可被理解"，内含"独立可理解、自成一体的陈述，并不附有其他可能解读或不可靠性的提示"。研究显示用户几乎从不点击 AI 概览中的来源链接，这进一步支持了法院的推理。

法院援引新闻法的类比：出版商对那些"单独即可理解"的引言也要负责，即便读者根本不会再读全文。法院还指出，如果 AI 概览"被普遍认为不可靠"，那么 Google 自己的论点反而会"显著削弱"该功能的价值。

法院还指出了一个保护漏洞：如果 Google 只对明显违法的情况承担责任，那么当 AI 作出虚假陈述时，受害人将没有真正可用的法律救济途径。被引用的第三方网站甚至从未作出相关陈述，因此受害人既无法起诉来源方，也难以有效起诉 Google。于是 Google 不能援引《数字服务法》对托管服务提供者的责任豁免，也无法套用搜索引擎的通知—删除流程。

### AI 生成的"意见"获得更少的言论自由保护

对 Google 来说雪上加霜的是，法院同时削减了 AI 生成内容的言论自由保护。法院写道：AI 的意见"并非表达者本人所形成的、被习得的信念，而是算法的结果"。提供 AI 驱动的检索"首先是 Google 商业活动的表达"，"至多是自由表达见解这一利益的一种次级表达"。在权衡原告的隐私权与 Google 的利益时，Google 须让位，尤其是争议陈述建立在不实事实之上——AI 把原告与一些公司挂钩，而根据具结书，这些公司与原告完全无关。

### Google 承担 80% 的诉讼费用

法院在大多数诉求上支持原告，禁止 Google 继续发布关于诈骗、与可疑公司关联、订阅陷阱、未发生过的来电、不可达等陈述；只有两项较小的诉求被驳回。即便特定文本已不再显示，重复侵权的风险仍然存在——Google 没有提交附处罚条款的停止侵害承诺，也没有任何机制阻止算法再次生成同样的陈述。Google 承担 80% 的诉讼费用，原告各承担 10%。该判决还可能具有跨国影响。

### 即便 91% 准确率也意味着上百万错误回答

慕尼黑判决的意义远不止这一桩案件。AI 创业公司 Oumi 为《纽约时报》所做的分析显示，使用当前 Gemini 3 模型的 Google AI Overviews 正确率为 91%。对大多数人的日常使用来说，这个数字已经不错；但按 Google 的体量，这意味着每小时仍有上百万条错误回答。如果其中足够多的内容构成对企业或个人的诽谤，可能成为法律层面的严重问题——不仅 Google，ChatGPT、Claude、Perplexity 等同类服务的提供商都在射程之内。

Oumi 的分析还发现，Gemini 3 的正确答案中有 56% 无法从 Google 链接的来源中得到印证。AI 正在给出用户无法追溯来源的回答。慕尼黑法院恰恰处理的就是这个问题：AI 作出原始链接中并不存在的自身陈述，运营商必须为之负责。这一推理在上诉中能否成立尚待观察，Google 也未对判决发表评论。但若该思路在国际上被采纳，冲击波将波及每一家靠改写网络内容来提供服务的 AI 厂商。
