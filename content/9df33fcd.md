Over the years I’ve read many blog posts from programmers giving advice on how to integrate (or make peace with) AI in software engineering. And, more often than not, the advice is written with the implicit assumption that the technology will stay where it was at the time of writing. So if you lay them out on a timeline, it might be:

-   “Tab completion is useful, but you need a human to have repository-wide context.”
-   “Coding agents are useful, but produce a lot of slop, you need to review and aggressively refactor their code.”
-   “Coding agents are very useful, but you still need to review the code.”
-   “Code was never the hard part. Actually the hard part is the deep tacit knowledge of talking to the customer.” (You are here)
-   “It is professional malpractice to write code by hand.”
-   “We have a cron job where every morning Claude Requiem one-shots the spec into x86 assembly.”

## English Summary

Fernando Borretti's Nov 14 essay pins down what taking AI seriously should mean. He arranges programmers' advice into a timeline from 'completion is useful but you must understand the whole codebase' to 'a cron job compiles the spec to x86 assembly every morning', and judges the trajectory reads like dispatches from a country losing a war. He then untangles 'AGI-pilled', which bundles two claims with very different evidence bases: short timelines and disruptive consequences. His falsifiable definition: taking AI seriously means believing progress may continue at recent-years rates - a conjecture data can overturn - while asserting progress will stop requires strictly weaker support.
