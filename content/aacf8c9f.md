# Radical responsibility means treating people like tools
> 原文链接: https://seangoedecke.com/radical-responsibility-means-treating-people-like-tools/

---

A lot of people think that good leadership requires **radical responsibility**. _[Conscious Leadership](https://conscious.is/resource/commitment-1-responsibility/)_ defines it like this:

> Taking full responsibility for one’s circumstances (physically, emotionally, mentally and spiritually) is the foundation of true personal and relational transformation. Conscious leadership and teams take full responsibility – radical responsibility – instead of placing blame. This means locating the cause and control of our lives in ourselves, not in external events.

Good leaders are outcome-focused. They are interested in actually succeeding, not in role-playing someone who’s trying to succeed[1](#fn-1). Placing blame is an excellent strategy for that kind of role-playing: if you can identify someone else who screwed up, you can claim to be trying to succeed no matter how badly you fail. When you look at people who are genuinely focused on winning, they don’t spend time blaming when things go wrong. They’re always focused on their _outs_: the remaining pathways to success, however few and narrow.

Never placing blame sounds like a kind thing to do. However, I think there’s something a little sociopathic about “radical responsibility”. If you assert _full_ responsibility for your own circumstances, then you’re necessarily not sharing responsibility with anyone else. That forces you to take an instrumental view of the people around you. They’re not peers who you might trust and be disappointed by; they’re either useful assets to be cultivated or useless liabilities to be handled or avoided[2](#fn-2). If you rely on someone and they let you down, that’s _your_ fault for being stupid enough to trust them in the first place. You ought to have made better tactical choices.

Many successful leaders adopt this mindset because it works. It really does help you win. But I don’t recommend it as a general approach to life. Trusting other people with responsibility is how you treat them like people, instead of like tools. And if you do that, you’ll sometimes assign praise and blame to them, instead of hoarding it all to yourself.

* * *

1.  In fact, many people role-play someone who’s trying to win as a substitute for actually trying. Here’s a relevant quote from Sartre’s _Being and Nothingness_: “The attentive pupil who wishes to be attentive, his eyes riveted on the teacher, his ears open wide, so exhausts himself in playing the attentive role that he ends up by no longer hearing anything.”
