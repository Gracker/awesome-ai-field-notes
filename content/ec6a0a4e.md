Get a demo Blog “Black October” for AI Developer Platforms A series of agentic attacks on AI Developer Platforms has been leading to LLMJacking at scale. Benjamin Read , Rami McCarthy October 30, 2026 | At least nine AI Developer Platforms have disclosed incidents over the course of the month: Fireworks AI (Oct 6) Andromeda (Oct 9) Zeabur (Oct 14) Lightning AI (Oct 15) OpenCode (Oct 16–17) Railway (Oct 20) Warp (Oct 23) CommandCode (Oct 28) Northflank (Oct 30) Wiz customers can refer to our relevant Threat Intel Center advisories for more information on the Railway Platform Breach, Lightning AI Security Incident and the overall campaign . Fireworks , OpenCode , and Warp have all reported indications of an “agent swarm” attack. Andromeda’s incident report calls it part of an "industry-wide attack." OpenCode and CommandCode have explicitly attributed their incidents to an attempt at “ distillation .” Any other month, and this would be the top security story. However, with all the ongoing incidents, few reports are connecting the dots across these attacks (Cloud Security Alliance as a notable exception ). The Pattern in these Attacks Currently, most of these security incidents are still being analyzed, and detailed technical information is not available. Zeabur has shared a full write-up, and the company will be shutting down due to this incident. Railway has released a preliminary report and timeline . However, there are some indicative details. Fireworks, Zeabur, and Lightning all attribute their incident root causes to credential access. Lightning was previously impacted by a supply chain attack within the Mini-Shai Hulud campaign . Railway so far has shared they believe the initial access was via “privilege escalation in our build infrastructure,” while Warp has suggested the root cause was a third-party vendor dependency, and OpenCode attributes it to a “legacy system.” All public reports highlight targeted exploitation, explicitly hunting stored environment variables, with a focus on those granting AI access. While there is no single vulnerability or dependency linking these attacks, the overall picture is strongly aligned with recent reports on two fronts: Agentic Attacks , which can enable threat actors to compromise victims faster and more efficiently. The rise of LLMJacking , and the associated infrastructure of LLM Brokers. These create a market that makes the theft easily monetizable, likely driving more actors to undertake operations to steal credentials. Distillation , notably alleged against Chinese AI labs, drives demand as well. Impact and Follow-on activity Reports on the impact of these attacks have so far been limited to anecdotal reports on social media, which include: Alleged theft of cryptocurrency, to the tune of ~$371,000 , using crypto keys stored in Railway Abuse of stolen AI access, such as those from OpenCode, resulting in consumer bills in the hundreds to thousands of dollars However, Wiz has been able to observe downstream abuse of enterprise credentials , whose compromise we can attribute to one of these vendor breaches. We will discuss an exemplary pair of such cases. In both cases, we see abuse of keys that previously were heavily used from the infrastructure of a compromised vendor. They shared an identical first unauthorized activity, which happened within 30 minutes of the reported credential access within the vendor breach. It was a single InvokeModel from a residential IP address, in a one-minute sweep that tested stolen keys across multiple organizations. The call asked for Claude Fable 5.1, failed, and fell back to Opus 5.5, which worked. In the days after this initial validation, both keys saw only a trickle of traffic: 14–120 calls a day from the same small Go client. The calls on the two keys ran in lockstep, about a minute apart. This indicates a single ongoing verification job checking that the keys remained valid. Case A Four days after the initial validation, a rotating proxy pool ran a catalog probe across dozens of addresses, sending one call per model across vendors: Amazon Nova, DeepSeek, gpt-oss, GLM, Kimi, Grok, GPT-6 Astra and older Claude models. Every call worked except Fable. A few hours later, a boto3 script on a residential address enumerated Bedrock and EC2 quotas. Twenty-two seconds after that, bulk traffic started from the same address. Within the next half hour, a second host mapped quotas and model availability across regions, making more than a thousand calls in under a minute. The Flood Over thirty hours, after which the key was remediated, the actor made approximately 800k Bedrock calls, and about 90% of them succeeded. The bulk ran first from the residential address and then, overnight, from a Level 3 hosting address. The key's policy allowed global.* cross-region inference profiles, and the actor routed every call through them. Because global routing spread the load across regions, only about 6% of calls were throttled. Traffic leaned heavily on Opus: Opus models were three quarters of successful calls, led by Opus 5 (36%), Opus 5.5 (22%) and Opus 4.8 (10%). Breakdown of LLMJacking token usage Case B Unlike Case A, this key was restricted. Seven days after the initial validation, it surfaced in an apparent reseller pool. Over four hours, thousands of failed calls came from rotating hosting addresses, each active for about 15 minutes. The calls failed because the key's policy allowed Bedrock only through us.* cross-region inference profiles, and the pool requested global.* . Early the next morning, a checker on a shared VPN exit identified the working us.* model IDs. Two hours later, a boto3 script on a Level 3 address retested global.* and us.* and enumerated Bedrock and EC2 quotas. It was the same script, down to the version string, that had probed Case A. Within minutes, the floodgates opened. The Flood Over thirteen hours, after which the key was remediated, that single Level 3 address made approximately 1.7 million Bedrock calls, with over a million successful. Nearly all of the rest were throttled: without global routing, the traffic ran into the account's regional quotas. Traffic was spread across a dozen Claude models and was less Opus-heavy than Case A. Opus 5.5, Sonnet 4.5 and Opus 5 made up three quarters of successful calls. Breakdown of LLMJacking token usage The Stolen Tokens In both cases, the token usage profile resembles resold coding-agent traffic. A typical call sent only a handful of uncached input tokens, the signature of prompt caching, and got back a few hundred tokens of output. A long tail of calls carried tens of thousands of uncached input tokens, consistent with many different end users behind a reseller. At list price, these two cases alone likely ran up over $100k in usage . What we see from these Attackers Our research has been able to link the usage of the keys in the cases above overt reselling organizations. While we are not able to definitively establish the relationship between these reselling organizations and either the initial compromise or the “flood” token abuse documented above, the actors had access to the compromised keys and tested them from their infrastructure. The use of the keys by multiple actors suggests a robust market for this type of credential. “momo” - Resale (and compromise?) Wiz Research identified an actor centered around 23.106.46.239 who appears to refer to themselves as “momo” who had access to the keys used in Case A and Case B and is tied to an overt LLM resale site.
